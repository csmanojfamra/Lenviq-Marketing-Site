"We wrote it off" is said as though it were the end of something. In the books it is: the asset
stops being carried. For the borrower it is not the end of anything at all — **the debt survives the
write-off completely**, and a lender who treats the two as the same thing stops chasing money it is
still owed.

That gap between what a write-off does to the balance sheet and what it does to the obligation is
the whole of this page.

## What the entry actually is

A write-off is the removal of a loan's carrying value from the books, charged against the provision
already held against it. On a fully provided account the profit and loss impact at write-off is
**nil** — the loss was taken when the provision was created, which is the point of provisioning.

That gives the sequence most lenders run:

1. Account turns non-performing. Accrued but uncollected interest is reversed to suspense; income
   moves to receipt basis ([what happens to income](/blog/npa-income-reversal/)).
2. Provisions build as the account ages through the classification bands.
3. At write-off, the carrying value goes against the provision.

**So a write-off is not when the loss happens.** It is the bookkeeping that follows a loss already
recognised. A lender who feels the write-off as the painful moment has probably been under-providing
on the way to it.

## What it does not do, and this is the operative list

**It does not extinguish the debt.** The borrower owes exactly what they owed the day before. No
contract is varied, no limitation period restarts, no security is released. A write-off is the
lender's accounting decision about its own balance sheet and the borrower is not a party to it.

**It does not end recovery.** The account continues to be pursued, and in most lenders it moves to a
recovery desk rather than out of the organisation. A written-off account with no owner is simply
money abandoned.

**It does not release the security.** A mortgage over property or a pledge of ornaments survives.
Releasing security because an account was written off is a separate — and usually wrong — decision.

**It does not clear the borrower's credit record.** The account is reported as written off, which is
worse for the borrower than an overdue account, not better. A borrower who believes a write-off
settles the matter is usually discovering otherwise at their next loan application.

**It does not make a settlement.** A one-time settlement is an agreement with the borrower that
releases them on agreed terms. A write-off is unilateral and releases nobody. These get conflated
constantly and they are opposites in the one respect that matters: consent.

## Recovery after write-off, which is where the books get interesting

Money recovered on a written-off account has nowhere to go in the normal way — there is no asset
left to reduce. It is recognised as income in the period it is received.

Two consequences follow, and both are operational rather than theoretical:

**The recovery has to be traceable to the written-off account.** If the account has been removed
from the loan book rather than marked, a receipt arrives with nothing to attach to, and either it
sits unapplied or it is posted to a suspense the next reconciliation has to explain. **A written-off
account must remain in the records as a written-off account** — the balance sheet stops carrying it;
the system does not stop knowing about it.

**Recovery flatters whichever period it lands in.** It is income without a matching cost in that
period, because the cost was taken earlier. Reading a month's profit without separating
written-off recovery from current-book income makes a bad month look ordinary.

## Prudential write-off, and the distinction worth keeping straight

A **prudential** or **technical** write-off removes the asset from the books while the lender
continues to pursue the borrower. It is an internal decision about the balance sheet.

A **waiver** or **settlement** gives something up contractually.

The two are often recorded in the same place and should not be. The question *"did we release the
borrower?"* has to be answerable from the record, because it determines whether the account can
still be pursued, what the borrower can be told, and what is reported.

## What the records have to be able to say

Four things, and a system that cannot answer any of them has a gap worth closing:

1. **That the account was written off, and on what date.** Not deleted — marked.
2. **What was written off, by component.** Principal, interest, charges. They are not
   interchangeable, and recovery is appropriated against them.
3. **Whether the borrower was released.** Write-off or settlement; the two must be distinguishable
   without reading a narration.
4. **Every recovery since**, attached to the written-off account rather than floating.

The fourth is the one most often missing, and it is the one that decides whether a recovery desk can
do its job at all.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a borrower still owe money after a loan is written off?

Yes, in full. A write-off is the lender's accounting treatment of its own balance sheet. It varies
no contract, releases no security and discharges no liability. Only a settlement or a waiver does
that, and both require an agreement with the borrower.

### Is a write-off the same as a one-time settlement?

No, and they are opposites in the respect that matters. A settlement is agreed with the borrower and
releases them on agreed terms. A write-off is unilateral and releases nobody.

### Does writing off a loan improve the borrower's credit record?

No. The account is reported as written off, which is a worse outcome on a credit record than an
account merely overdue.

### What happens to money recovered on a written-off account?

There is no asset left to reduce, so it is recognised as income in the period received. It should be
reported separately from current-book income, because it is income with no matching cost in that
period and it will otherwise flatter the month it lands in.

### Should a written-off account be removed from the system?

No. The balance sheet stops carrying it; the records must not stop knowing about it. An account
deleted rather than marked leaves later recoveries with nothing to attach to.
