Tamil Nadu and Kerala both cap **what a money-lender may charge**. West Bengal, in the main, does
something else: it caps **what a court will let the lender recover**. The [Bengal Money-Lenders Act, 1940](https://cdn.s3waas.gov.in/s35487315b1286f907165907aa8fc96619/uploads/2025/09/17573135855085.pdf)
is older than both, it is built around the suit rather than the licence counter, and a lender
who reads it expecting the southern pattern will mis-plan in three separate places.

**Read the sourcing note at the end before relying on a rate.** It is weaker here than on the
[Tamil Nadu](/blog/tamil-nadu-money-lending-licence/) and
[Kerala](/blog/kerala-money-lending-licence/) pages, and the reason is stated plainly.

## Who needs a licence, and who issues it

**Section 8 — "Money-lending business not to be carried on except under licence."** After a date
the State Government notifies — not less than six months after the Act's commencement — a
money-lender may not carry on the business unless he holds an **effective licence**. The Explanation
defines that: a licence issued to a person who is **not disqualified** from holding one.

That phrasing does work. A licence in your hand that was issued to a disqualified person is not an
effective licence, so the section is not satisfied by possession alone.

**Section 6** appoints the Registrars. In Kolkata the proviso to section 6 empowers the **Collector
of Kolkata (Stamp & Revenue)** to issue licences within the limits of the 78 police stations under
the Commissioner of Police, Kolkata; elsewhere it is the Sub-Registrar.

**Section 7** requires each Sub-Registrar to maintain, **in the prescribed form, a register of the
money-lenders holding licences issued by him.** The register is the State's, not the lender's — a
different thing from the books in section 24.

## The licence is State-wide, and this is the big structural difference

**Section 9(1): a licence is valid throughout the whole of West Bengal, for three years from the
date of issue, unless cancelled earlier.** **Section 9(2):** on expiry or cancellation it must be
**returned to the Sub-Registrar who issued it**.

Compare what that replaces. Tamil Nadu (s.3(2)) and Kerala (s.3(2)) both require **a separate
licence for each shop or place of business**. West Bengal requires one licence, valid across the
State, renewed on a three-year cycle.

So a lender opening a second branch faces three different answers in three states: a second licence
in Tamil Nadu, a second licence in Kerala, and nothing further in West Bengal. This is the single
most expensive assumption to carry across a border.

And the obligation to **hand the licence back** has no southern equivalent. It is a small
administrative act that is easy to miss on cancellation, which is exactly when attention is
elsewhere.

## The books a West Bengal money-lender must keep

**Section 24 — "Duty of money-lender to keep accounts."**

**Section 24(1):** a money-lender must keep **at least a cash book, a ledger and a receipt book**,
each in the form prescribed under the Act, and written in **Bengali or English**.

Three named books, not a general obligation to keep accounts — and a prescribed form for each, which
means the forms are in the rules rather than in the Act.

**Section 24(2)(a):** the statement of the loan given to the borrower at the time of the advance must
be in **Bengali or English, whichever the borrower chooses.** The borrower picks, not the lender.

**Section 24(2)(b):** for every payment towards a loan, the lender must give **"a plain and complete
receipt"** at the time of payment.

**Section 25** separately requires **annual statements of account** to borrowers — an obligation that
arises without the borrower asking, which neither Tamil Nadu nor Kerala has. In both of those, the
statement follows a written requisition from the debtor.

So the West Bengal package is: three prescribed books, a loan statement in the borrower's chosen
language at the time of the advance, a receipt at the time of each payment, and an annual statement
whether or not anybody asks.

## Section 30, which is where this Act actually bites

**"Limitations as to amount and rate of interest recoverable."** It applies notwithstanding any
other law in force or any agreement — so it is not contractable around — and since the 1981
amendment, commercial loans are carved out into a separate section 30A.

**Section 30(1)** sets three limits on what a borrower is liable to pay:

- **(a)** principal and interest together, counting amounts already paid or already included in a
  decree, may not exceed **twice the principal of the original loan**;
- **(b)** interest outstanding may not exceed the **principal outstanding** on the computation date;
- **(c)** only **simple interest** is recoverable, at rates capped by whether the loan is secured or
  unsecured.

**Sub-section (2)** applies the same rate limits retrospectively to interest paid, or included in a
decree, before commencement. **Sub-section (3)** preserves the lender's right to sue.

**(a) is the rule worth internalising.** It is the old *damdupat* principle in statutory form: a loan
of ₹1,00,000 cannot be made to yield more than ₹2,00,000 in total, however long it runs and whatever
the contract says. A lender pricing a long-tenure book in West Bengal on an assumed yield has a
ceiling that no rate card changes.

**(b) stops interest overtaking principal** at any point on the computation date, which is a tighter
constraint than (a) on a loan that is being serviced slowly.

**The rates in (c).** Simple interest, capped at **12.5% per annum on an unsecured loan and 10% on a
secured one**. Two independent reproductions of the Act agree on both figures, and one carries the
provenance: sub-clause (c) was **substituted by section 6 of the Bengal Money-lenders (Amendment)
Act, 1965 (West Bengal Act XXI of 1965)**. That settles an ambiguity worth naming — one
reproduction renders this sub-clause with old and current text run together, and the 1965
substitution is the current limb.

**Section 30A, for commercial loans, is a different pair of numbers: 20% unsecured and 17%
secured.** Which section applies therefore turns on whether the borrowing is commercial, and that
is the first question to settle rather than the last.

## What actually happens to an unlicensed lender, and it is not the fine

This is the part that most repays reading, because the criminal penalty is almost nothing and the
commercial consequence is total.

**Section 42 — general penalties**, which apply where the Act provides no specific penalty: a
money-lender, or a servant, agent or manager of the business, who **knowingly and wilfully**
commits, authorises or permits a default or contravention. **First offence: fine up to ₹200. Second:
up to ₹500. Subsequent: rigorous imprisonment up to three months, with a fine.** Liability reaches
members of an undivided Hindu family, directors and officers of a body corporate, and members of an
unincorporated body who knowingly and wilfully take part.

Those figures are from 1940 and have not kept pace with anything. On their own they would make the
Act look toothless.

**Section 13 is where the teeth are, and it is civil.** A court **may not pass a decree or order in
a money-lender's favour** in a suit to recover a loan — or to enforce an agreement or security
connected with it — unless satisfied that the lender held an **effective licence when the loan, or
any part of it, was advanced**.

And if the court finds at trial that no licence was held, it must, before going further, **order the
lender to pay a penalty "as the Court thinks fit", up to three times the licence fee** under section
10. **Unpaid within the time allowed, and the suit is dismissed.** Paid, and it proceeds.

Section 13 also reaches **set-off claims**, and **assignees** where the assignment was made to avoid
the licence fee and the penalty — so the obvious workaround is written out of it.

**Read together: lending without a licence in West Bengal is a ₹200 offence and an unrecoverable
book.** A lender can run unlicensed for years at trivial criminal risk and discover, the first time
a borrower defaults and the matter reaches a court, that nothing is enforceable. The Calcutta High
Court set aside a decree in favour of an unlicensed lender on exactly this ground in 2024.

It is the same design as section 30, one step further: West Bengal regulates money lending through
**what a court will do**, not through what an inspector will fine.

## Three states, three different regimes

| | Tamil Nadu (1957) | Kerala (1958) | West Bengal (1940) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Licence covers | each shop or place of business | each shop or place of business | **the whole State** |
| Validity | — | — | **three years**, returnable on expiry |
| Ceiling is on | what you may **charge** — notification, correlated to RBI bank rates | what you may **charge** — 2% above the maximum commercial bank rate, in the Act | what a court will let you **recover** — twice the principal, simple interest only |
| Books | account per **debtor**, incl. nature of security | account per **debt** | **cash book, ledger, receipt book**, prescribed form |
| Language | prescribed per area | — | **Bengali or English**, borrower's choice for the loan statement |
| Statement to borrower | on written requisition | on written requisition | **annually, unasked** (s.25) |
| Unlicensed lending — criminal | fine up to ₹1,000 | **imprisonment, min. three months**, to three years, fine up to ₹50,000 | fine up to **₹200**, then ₹500, then rigorous imprisonment up to 3 months (s.42) |
| Unlicensed lending — commercial | — | — | **no decree at all** (s.13): penalty up to 3× the licence fee, and the suit is **dismissed** if unpaid |

The row that most often costs money is the third. A lender who has planned for a charging cap walks
into West Bengal and finds the constraint sitting on recovery instead — it does not bite until the
suit, and by then the book is written.

Three things to settle against the gazette before acting:

1. **The section 30(1)(c) rates**, for the reason given above.
2. **Amendments after West Bengal Act IV of 1981.** The most recent amendment any source lists.
3. **That the 1940 Act is still the operative statute.** One secondary source refers to a "West
   Bengal Money-Lenders Act, 1993". I looked for it and found nothing, and the Kolkata
   Collectorate publishes the **1940** Act as the law it licenses under — so the 1993 reference
   appears to be an error. Recorded because a reader may meet the same claim.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a West Bengal money lender need a licence for each branch?

No. Under section 9(1) a licence is valid throughout the whole of West Bengal for three years from
issue. That is the opposite of Tamil Nadu and Kerala, where section 3(2) of each Act requires a
separate licence for every shop or place of business.

### What is the maximum a lender can recover in West Bengal?

Section 30(1)(a) limits principal and interest together — including amounts already paid or included
in a decree — to twice the principal of the original loan. Section 30(1)(b) separately stops interest
outstanding exceeding principal outstanding, and (c) allows simple interest only.

### What books must a West Bengal money lender keep?

Section 24(1): at least a cash book, a ledger and a receipt book, each in the prescribed form and
written in Bengali or English. Section 24(2)(b) requires a plain and complete receipt at the time of
every payment, and section 25 requires annual statements of account to borrowers.

### Who issues the licence in Kolkata?

The Collector of Kolkata (Stamp & Revenue), empowered by the proviso to section 6, within the limits
of the 78 police stations under the Commissioner of Police, Kolkata. Elsewhere in the State it is the
Sub-Registrar, who also maintains the section 7 register of licence holders.

### What happens if a West Bengal money lender has no licence?

Very little criminally — section 42 gives a fine up to ₹200 for a first offence, ₹500 for a second,
and rigorous imprisonment up to three months thereafter. The real consequence is section 13: a court
may not pass a decree in the lender's favour unless an effective licence was held when the loan was
advanced, must order a penalty of up to three times the licence fee on finding there was none, and
must dismiss the suit if that penalty goes unpaid. An unlicensed book is an unrecoverable one.

### Does section 30 apply to business borrowing?

Not in the same way. Commercial loans sit under section 30A, which allows **20% per annum unsecured
and 17% secured** against section 30's 12.5% and 10%. So whether the borrowing is commercial is the
first question, not the last.
